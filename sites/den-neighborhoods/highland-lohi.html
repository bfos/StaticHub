Overview

Highland and LoHi (Lower Highland) are technically two sub-neighborhoods within Denver's broader Highlands area, but they function as one cohesive destination. LoHi is the eastern, lower-elevation portion — directly across the Millennium Bridge from Union Station, with the highest concentration of restaurants, bars, and new construction. West Highland and Highland proper extend west and north from there, with more residential streets and the older Highland Square commercial district around 32nd and Lowell.

This is the most urban neighborhood in this guide — and deliberately so. If you said you're "willing to go more urban than PV if the neighborhood has the right character," Highland/LoHi is the test case. The character is extraordinary: Victorian-era homes on steep streets with mountain and skyline views, one of Denver's densest concentrations of exceptional restaurants, and a walkability score that rivals neighborhoods in larger coastal cities. But it reads urban first and residential second, which is a real distinction from your PV/Brookside baseline.

The demographic mix in Highland (57% white, 37% Hispanic) is more diverse than most neighborhoods in this guide — a remnant of its working-class Latino heritage, which is being steadily gentrified but not yet erased. LoHi itself skews younger and more affluent.

Culture & Feel

LoHi's restaurant and bar scene is genuinely exceptional — this is where serious Denver food people eat. A sampling of the best-known spots: Linger (a converted mortuary turned globally-inspired rooftop restaurant — legitimately one of Denver's best), El Five (Spanish/Mediterranean rooftop, mountain views, among the most beautiful dining rooms in Denver), Root Down (farm-to-table pioneer, still excellent), The Populist (intimate, locally-sourced American), Avanti Food & Beverage (a collective food hall with multiple vendors — the KC Crossroads version of a food hall but better-executed).

For drinks: the cocktail culture in LoHi is sophisticated. Williams & Graham (a speakeasy-style bar consistently ranked among America's best cocktail bars) is nearby. Multiple craft brewery taprooms are within walking distance including Odell Brewing's downtown taproom. The bar scene runs later here than in Wash Park or Platt Park, and it feels more like a night-out destination and less like a neighborhood pub.

Highland Square (32nd and Lowell) provides the more residential commercial district feel — coffee shops, a bookstore, an old-school diner — that PV residents will find familiar. It's calmer and more neighborhood-scaled than LoHi proper, while sharing the same walkable residential streets.

Biking is an exceptional option: the Millennium Bridge to Union Station takes under five minutes, from which you can access Coors Field, the 16th Street Mall, and the entire LoDo bar/restaurant scene. For someone who enjoys biking as a primary transport mode, LoHi's position is unmatched in Denver.

Kansas City Comparables

Highland/LoHi maps most closely onto the Crossroads Arts District of KC, but considerably more residential and more livable. The restaurant-and-bar energy is pure Crossroads — concentration of excellent independent food and beverage, design-forward spaces, young professional crowd. But where Crossroads is primarily a destination district that people drive to, LoHi is a neighborhood you actually live in. The residential streets of West Highland — 32nd Avenue, Grove Street, Irving Street — feel more like Volker: Victorian-era homes, mature trees, genuine neighborhood identity. Imagine Volker and Crossroads compressed together, separated by a five-minute bike ride.

Honest Tradeoffs for the PV/Brookside Sensibility
  • This neighborhood reads urban in a way that PV does not. If you want the quiet residential street with a car-free Saturday morning walk to the bakery, LoHi delivers it — but you'll also have rooftop bar noise on Friday nights and weekend evening pedestrian traffic. The urban energy is the point, but it is present 24/7.
  • New construction townhomes and condos are extremely common in LoHi, especially near the bridge. If you want a historic home on a residential street, you need to look specifically in West Highland — the supply is more limited and the architecture more varied (some Victorian originals, some Denver Squares, some 1950s ranches).
  • Traffic and parking are genuinely problematic near the bridge and Avanti/restaurant cluster on weekend evenings. Living one block from the scene is great; living directly on it less so.
  • The neighborhood skews young (median age 33) and the social gravity of LoHi is heavily oriented toward people in their 20s and 30s without kids. You'll find plenty of your peers, but it's not a neighborhood where you'll bump into empty-nesters at the farmers market.
  • Price compression: LoHi prices have been volatile. The market saw significant corrections in late 2025; you may find good value, but the townhouse-heavy inventory means you're often competing on a product type (attached, multi-story, rooftop deck) that may not be what you're looking for.

Typical Homes & Architecture

Highland's historic housing stock is primarily Victorian-era: Queen Anne cottages, Italianate rowhouses, and early foursquares from the 1880s–1910s. These are the originals, and in good condition they're stunning — elaborate woodwork, steep gables, deep front porches. Prices for restored Victorians start around $800K.

LoHi itself has seen significant infill: modern townhomes (3–4 stories, rooftop decks, 2-car garages, attached) dominate the newer inventory. These are well-designed and functionally excellent but architecturally jarring against their older neighbors. Prices $850K–$1.3M for new townhomes.
