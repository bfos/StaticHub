Overview

Berkeley is a city-center neighborhood in Northwest Denver, bounded by Federal Boulevard on the east, I-70 on the north, Sheridan Boulevard on the west, and 38th Avenue on the south. It sits directly north of the Highland/LoHi area and is sometimes (incorrectly) grouped with "the Highlands" by real estate marketers — but it has a distinct identity rooted in Tennyson Street and its two parks (Berkeley Lake and Rocky Mountain Lake).

5280 Magazine ranked Berkeley as Denver's #1 Best Neighborhood in 2025, and the reasoning is easy to understand. Tennyson Street (38th to 46th Avenues) has developed over the past decade into one of Denver's most compelling neighborhood commercial corridors: 60+ independent businesses with almost zero chain presence, a monthly First Friday art walk, the historic Oriental Theatre, and a restaurant scene that's earned national attention.

Berkeley was originally a working-class neighborhood — annexed by Denver in 1902 — with a history of immigrant families. Like many Northwest Denver neighborhoods, it has gentrified significantly over the past two decades while retaining more economic diversity than Wash Park or Platt Park. The housing stock is modest in scale (bungalows, ranches, Denver Squares) but the condition of well-maintained examples is excellent, and prices have risen accordingly.

Culture & Feel

Tennyson Street is Berkeley's identity. On a Saturday afternoon it functions like a neighborhood main street in the best sense: people walking, restaurants with outdoor seating, a coffee shop where regulars know each other by name, galleries open for browsing, and zero chain stores. The monthly First Friday art walk has been running since the early 2000s and remains one of Denver's most genuine community events — not curated or branded, just a neighborhood where businesses open their doors and people show up.

The food and drink scene is anchored by Hops & Pie (artisanal pizza with an exceptional rotating craft beer list — consistently one of Denver's best neighborhood restaurants), Denver Biscuit Co. (a landmark breakfast spot), and Bardo Coffee House (the neighborhood's living room). The Oriental Theatre at 44th and Tennyson hosts national touring acts, comedy, and community events in a lovingly preserved 1927 movie palace.

Berkeley Lake and Rocky Mountain Lake provide the neighborhood's green space — both are genuine parks with paths, picnic areas, and water that waterfowl use year-round. They're smaller than Wash Park but far less crowded, and on a weekday morning they're serene in a way the more famous parks rarely are.

The resident mix skews toward young professionals in their late 20s–30s and families with young children — Berkeley has long had a reputation as a neighborhood where artists and creative professionals land when LoHi becomes too expensive. That's still broadly true, which gives it a more eclectic social mix than Wash Park or Platt Park.

Kansas City Comparables

Berkeley maps most closely to the energy of West Plaza and Waldo in KC — a neighborhood commercial strip with genuine independent culture, not quite as established as Brookside but with arguably more creative energy. The Tennyson Street First Friday art walk is essentially the KC First Fridays in the Crossroads, but embedded within a residential neighborhood rather than an arts district. The housing stock (1920s–1940s bungalows) is pure Waldo/West Plaza. Berkeley also has some of the character of Westwood — a neighborhood that has risen faster than people expected, driven by restaurants and walkability, and now punching above its price weight.

Honest Tradeoffs for the PV/Brookside Sensibility
  • Berkeley is further from central Denver than Wash Park or LoHi — about 4–5 miles to LoDo/downtown. It's not far, but the drive through Northwest Denver traffic is real. Biking is good but not as effortless as LoHi's bridge connection.
  • The neighborhood sits directly under the flight path for Denver International Airport. Noise is intermittent but real, particularly during certain wind patterns when aircraft stack up on approach. It's not a deal-breaker for most residents but worth knowing.
  • The median household income (~$95K) is lower than Wash Park or Platt Park, which means the neighborhood is more economically mixed. This is a positive for some; for others expecting the PV income/lifestyle homogeneity, it's an adjustment.
  • Home sizes in Berkeley tend toward the modest end — bungalows of 900–1,400 sq ft predominate. Finding something over 1,800 sq ft often means looking at remodeled properties with rear additions, which can be well done or awkward.
  • Tennyson Street is very popular on weekends; parking is competitive. But this is, again, the Brookside problem: success creates congestion, and Berkeley has had the same success.

Typical Homes & Architecture

Berkeley's housing stock is primarily 1910s–1940s craftsman bungalows: single-story, low-pitched gabled roofs, wide front porches, brick or stucco exteriors. There are also some Denver Squares (two-and-a-half story foursquares), 1940s–1950s ranches, and a small number of new infill townhomes (primarily on corners or where older structures were demolished). Lot sizes average 6,000 sq ft. Alley garages are standard.
