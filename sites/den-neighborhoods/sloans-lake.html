Overview

Sloan's Lake is defined by its extraordinary central feature: a 177-acre lake surrounded by a 290-acre park, the second largest in Denver after City Park. The neighborhood wraps around the lake on its south and east sides, with Edgewater (a small incorporated municipality) on the west and Wheat Ridge to the north. The approximate boundaries are Sheridan Boulevard to the west, 17th Avenue to the south, Raleigh Street to the east, and 26th Avenue to the north.

The neighborhood has undergone dramatic change since 2010. The closure and demolition of St. Anthony's Hospital opened seven blocks to redevelopment, which brought a 12-story condo tower (Lakehouse), a 369-unit apartment building, and a new strip of restaurants and shops along 17th Avenue. The pre-existing bungalow stock has appreciated accordingly — average prices rose from $259K in 2009 to over $885K today. The neighborhood feels like it's in transition between its working-class origins and its current more affluent identity, which creates a range of housing options and some neighborhood texture that more fully-arrived neighborhoods lack.

More than 66% of Sloan's Lake residents own their homes — an above-average figure for Denver that speaks to the neighborhood's stability and community investment.

Culture & Feel

Sloan's Lake's identity is organized around the park. The 2.6-mile perimeter path is one of Denver's most-used running and biking loops, with the Rockies visible to the west on clear days. The annual Dragon Boat Festival each summer brings tens of thousands of visitors. White pelicans, herons, and osprey use the lake year-round, and an osprey nesting platform on Penny Island has become something of a local landmark.

The brewery scene along the park's southern edge and in adjacent Edgewater is exceptional. Odell Brewing Co.'s Sloan's Lake Brewhouse (one block from the park) is a significant outpost from one of Colorado's most respected craft brewers. Joyride Brewing Company across Sheridan in Edgewater has a beloved dog-friendly rooftop patio. Hogshead Brewery does English-style ales with a comfortable neighborhood pub feel. The overall effect is a walkable beer-culture corridor around the lake's southern perimeter that rivals Berkeley's Tennyson Street for casual evenings.

Restaurant options have expanded with the St. Anthony's redevelopment: new spots along 17th Avenue include a mix of breakfast, casual dining, and destination restaurants. The adjacent Edgewater Public Market (just across Sheridan in Edgewater) functions as a food hall with multiple vendors — a genuine neighborhood amenity. West 29th Avenue, the neighborhood's main commercial strip on the east side, has coffee shops, wine bars, and a mix of neighborhood-serving retail.

The atmosphere around the park is active and community-oriented without being loud or tourist-driven. Weekend mornings — people running, cycling, walking dogs, picnicking by the water — feel like exactly the active-lifestyle neighborhood you're describing. The mountain views on clear days from the western park path are exceptional.

Kansas City Comparables

Sloan's Lake maps most closely to the Loose Park / Ward Parkway area of KC — specifically the residential streets immediately adjacent to a large, signature park. The park-centered social life (the running loop, the lake, the weekend picnics) is almost identical in character to how Brookside residents use Loose Park. The brewery scene along the park's perimeter is similar in energy to what Westwood is developing, but more established. In physical character, the 1920s bungalows near the lake feel like quieter Waldo blocks — well-maintained, modest in scale, and increasingly expensive.

Honest Tradeoffs for the PV/Brookside Sensibility
  • Sloan's Lake walkability is good (76) but not exceptional by Denver's best standards. Most daily errands still require a car, and the commercial options within the neighborhood itself are more limited than Platt Park or Berkeley. You'll drive for most dining and shopping.
  • The neighborhood is still actively transitioning. Some blocks feel like the 2026 version; others still have the pre-gentrification working-class character. This creates value opportunity but also means quality is uneven.
  • New construction along the lake's south shore (condos, apartment buildings) has added density that changes the feel of those blocks. If you want the bungalow-neighborhood experience, focus on streets to the north and east of the park, away from the redevelopment.
  • The lake attracts significant weekend visitor traffic from all over Denver, which means park crowding and parking competition on summer weekends. This is amplified by the Dragon Boat Festival and summer concerts.
  • Location is slightly further from central Denver's amenities than LoHi or Wash Park — positioned at the city's west edge, with Edgewater and Lakewood directly to the west. The further-west blocks can feel more suburban-adjacent.

Typical Homes & Architecture

The housing stock in Sloan's Lake is among Denver's most diverse: 1920s brick bungalows (the neighborhood's historic character), 1940s–1950s ranches, 1960s split-levels, and a significant amount of new construction including the Lakehouse condo tower and infill townhomes around the St. Anthony's site. This variety means more options at different price points, but also more visual inconsistency than neighborhoods with tighter architectural periods.

Bungalows near the park (north of 17th, south of 26th, between Raleigh and Sheridan) represent the neighborhood's best residential streetscapes. Prices for well-maintained examples run $800K–$1.1M. The new condos start around $700K for smaller units.
