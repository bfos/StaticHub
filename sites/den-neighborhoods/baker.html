Overview

Baker is one of Denver's oldest and most historically significant neighborhoods. Approximately one mile south of the Civic Center, it's bounded by West 6th Avenue on the north, Broadway on the east, West Mississippi Avenue on the south, and the South Platte River on the west. More than 80% of the neighborhood was developed before 1900, and a 150-acre historic district in the northeast corner was added to the National Register of Historic Places in 1985.

Baker contains the largest concentration of middle-class Victorian Queen Anne homes in Denver — 19th-century brick houses on a scale unlike anything else in the city. The neighborhood includes 39 buildings designed by William Lang, Denver's most prominent late-Victorian architect. This is authentic historic fabric, not a reconstruction — these are working-class and middle-class homes from the 1880s–1900s that have survived and, in recent decades, been lovingly restored.

Baker's demographics have shifted significantly since 2000, when approximately 54% of residents identified as Hispanic or Latino. By 2020 that figure was 18% — a clear indicator of gentrification that accelerated through the 2010s. The neighborhood is now younger (median age 32), whiter, and more educated than its historical average, though it retains a more economically mixed character than Wash Park or Platt Park.

Culture & Feel

Baker's energy comes from two sources: the residential Victorian core and South Broadway's bar/restaurant corridor. These are genuinely distinct experiences separated by a few blocks.

The residential streets — Santa Fe Drive, Elati, Cherokee, Bannock, between 1st and 6th Avenues — are among Denver's most atmospheric. Victorian-era homes set back from brick sidewalks, mature street trees (elms and locusts that survived Denver's Dutch elm disease losses), painted ladies and restored Italianates side by side. On a weekday morning, walking these blocks feels more like an older American city than any other neighborhood in Denver. There is visual complexity and historic character that the city's newer neighborhoods simply cannot manufacture.

South Broadway (the eastern edge of Baker) is a different scene: Denver's most eclectic and enduring bar corridor. The stretch from 1st Avenue to Evans has been a going-out destination since at least the 1980s. My Brother's Bar (Denver's oldest bar, established 1873) is nearby; The Hornet has been a neighborhood institution for decades. Newer additions include craft cocktail bars, music venues, a vinyl record shop, and multiple restaurants. The overall character is eclectic and a bit rough around the edges — more Waldo/39th Street than Brookside, intentionally so.

The Santa Fe Arts District directly borders Baker's western edge — more than 40 galleries and studios concentrated on Santa Fe Drive between 6th and Colfax. The First Friday art walk draws thousands of visitors from across Denver, and several galleries have achieved national recognition. This is a genuine arts district, not a branded one.

Baker has excellent transit (Walk Score 85, Transit 72) by Denver standards — two light rail stations (Alameda and I-25/Broadway) serve it, making it among the most transit-connected residential neighborhoods in the city. This also means access to downtown without a car — a rarity in Denver.

Kansas City Comparables

Baker maps most closely to the Crossroads Arts District of KC, but considerably more residential — think Crossroads compressed into a neighborhood you actually live in rather than drive to. The Victorian architecture is closer to KC's Hyde Park area — genuinely late-19th-century residential fabric at scale. The South Broadway bar corridor feels like 39th Street or early Westport — an eclectic mix of longstanding bars and newer spots on a one-way commercial strip. Baker is grittier and more urban than anything in PV/Brookside, and that's a feature for some buyers and not for others. If you've always secretly wished Brookside had more grit and history, Baker might be your neighborhood.

Honest Tradeoffs for the PV/Brookside Sensibility
  • Baker is significantly grittier and more urban than PV or Brookside. The neighborhood borders industrial uses near the Platte, and the South Broadway corridor has dive bars, pawn shops, and adult establishments alongside excellent restaurants. If you want PV's sanitized polish, Baker will feel raw.
  • The neighborhood's rapid gentrification has created some social tension. The dramatic demographic shift from 54% Hispanic (2000) to 18% Hispanic (2020) is visible in the community's character and has generated legitimate concerns about displacement. This is the lived reality of many Denver neighborhoods, but it's particularly stark here.
  • The median income (~$75K) is the lowest of any neighborhood in this guide — Baker is not yet a fully affluent neighborhood, and the mixed economic character shows. Some blocks are beautifully maintained; others have deferred maintenance and more transient residents.
  • Parking is genuinely difficult. South Broadway's bars draw visitors who park on residential streets. There is street parking resident permit zoning, but weekend evenings in Baker are not quiet.
  • Two major stadium developments (Denver Summit FC women's soccer, and potentially a new Broncos stadium at Burnham Yard to the north) are planned for Baker's edges through 2028–2030. These will bring construction disruption before they bring neighborhood amenity — and how they ultimately affect the neighborhood character is genuinely uncertain.

Typical Homes & Architecture

Baker's housing stock is unique in Denver: the neighborhood's age (80%+ developed by 1900) means that Victorian-era construction — brick Queen Annes, Italianates, and Craftsman cottages — dominates the residential streets. These are typically 1,200–2,000 sq ft on 3,000–6,000 sq ft lots, often with small rear yards and no garages. The historic district designation protects the neighborhood's character and prevents most new infill that would disrupt the scale.

Restored Victorians in good condition run $600K–$850K. Unremodeled properties can be found below $600K (rare) but often require significant investment. A few newer townhome developments near the Broadway corridor start around $500K for attached units.
