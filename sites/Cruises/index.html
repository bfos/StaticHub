A second try at sea

Five lines that fix what went wrong on the Discovery Princess — built around relaxed luxury, real service, and a family of four with one adult daughter and one sixteen-year-old.

7 nights Caribbean Party of 4 · ages 16 & 18 Target: well under $11k all-in

The brief

What we're solving for

Last year's cruise was your most expensive trip and your lowest-rated. The ports were fine — the ship let you down. The fixes below all trace back to moving up one tier and choosing a warmer route.

What disappointed you

Mediocre food, slow service (15–30 min for a single app-ordered drink), a drink package you lost ~$150 each on, lame entertainment, a smoking casino, dinner-line waits, and pools too cool to enjoy.

foodservicevalueentertainmentsmokecool pools

What you actually want

Quiet luxury. Reading by the pool, music, and food and drink that arrives without a fight. No water-slides, characters, crooners, or cheesy lessons. Worth paying a bit more for an experience that beats a staycation.

The party

Two adults, one daughter 18+, one daughter 16. The 16-year-old keeps the fully adults-only lines (Virgin, Oceania, Viking) off the table — every option here welcomes a teen.

Two settings worth changing

Sail the Caribbean, not the Pacific — your cool-pool problem was the Mexican-Riviera route, not bad luck. And step up one tier: every complaint above is a mass-market-staffing symptom.

The shortlist, in detail

Two ways to step up — and the splurges

There are two genuinely different moves above Celebrity, both teen-friendly. A luxury enclave gives you a private bubble on a big ship; an all-inclusive luxury line is smaller and quieter with near 1:1 service. Then there are the splurges worth knowing about.

MSC Yacht Club

Luxury enclave · best fit

Est. $7,000–$11,000 · all-inclusive

A keycard-only enclave of suites with a private restaurant, pool, sundeck, lounge and a 24/7 butler — while the full ship outside has the energy and teen spaces your 16-year-old wants. Premium drinks, WiFi and tips are all in the fare, and the butler model is the structural cure for the slow service that defined your last trip.

✓

Teens: full run of the main ship and its teen venues; can't enter Yacht Club areas unless booked in it.

Your question, answered

Can you book luxury for the parents and standard rooms for the teens?

Short answer: not in the clean way you're picturing — and it's the very same wall you hit on Princess. It isn't a Princess quirk. It's an industry-wide minor-safety rule, and your 16-year-old is the one it catches.

The rule (US-port Caribbean sailings): every guest under 21 must be in the same, connecting, or directly-adjacent stateroom as a guest aged 21+. A guest under 18 (your 16-year-old) specifically must adjoin a parent's cabin. Because luxury enclaves like the Yacht Club and the Haven are physically separate, access-controlled zones, a standard cabin can't be booked next to an enclave suite — so the split breaks.

Line-by-line on "parents in the enclave, both teens in standard cabins":

Not as-is
MSC Yacht Club. Yacht Club access is members-only and the area is sealed off, so your 16-year-old can't be placed in an adjacent standard cabin. Notably, MSC does let an 18–20-year-old have her own standalone standard cabin as long as a 21+ guest is on the booking — so the 18-year-old is flexible; the 16-year-old is the constraint.
Not as-is
Norwegian Haven. Both daughters are under 21, and NCL requires under-21 guests to be in the same or a connecting/adjoining cabin to a 21+ adult. The Haven is a gated complex, so neither daughter can sit in a standard cabin elsewhere on the ship.
Not reliably
Celebrity Retreat. Same logic — under-21 guests must be next door or across the hall from a parent. Suite layouts don't put bookable standard cabins across from Retreat suites, so the split isn't dependable.

What you can do instead

The efficient enclave version (MSC). Put the parents and the 16-year-old together in one Yacht Club suite — many sleep three — and give the 18-year-old her own inexpensive standard cabin. You pay enclave rates for three, not four, and the adult daughter gets full independence cheaply.
The simplest, cheapest split (Celebrity, no enclave). Book two connecting or adjacent balcony cabins — a parent paired with a daughter in each. Easy to book, maximum flexibility, and each cabin satisfies the 21+ rule. You give up the butler bubble but keep the upgrade and the separate teen rooms.
The whole family in the bubble. Two adjacent Yacht Club or Haven cabins. Everyone gets the luxury — revisit only if the budget stretches, since it's the most expensive path.

Worth noting: the day your younger daughter turns 18 (and ideally 21), this split becomes trivial on every line. For this trip, option 1 or 2 gets you closest to the efficient setup you wanted.

The recommendation

Where I'd point you

For the experience you described — quiet luxury, real service, food and drink that simply arrive — and a teen who still wants a fun ship around her, MSC Yacht Club is the standout. It hands the parents a genuine butler-served, all-inclusive luxury bubble while keeping a lively ship for your 16-year-old, often at a price near a Celebrity suite. Book the parents and your 16-year-old in one Yacht Club suite and let the 18-year-old take her own standard cabin.

If you'd rather keep the spend comfortably under budget, Celebrity's Always Included on an Edge-class ship is the safe, proven step up — better food, a smoke-free casino, and two connecting balconies that give the girls their own room without any policy headaches.

Look at Regent only if you decide to splurge and like that one number covers excursions, drinks, tips and maybe air — its near 1:1 service is the real antidote to everything slow about last year.

Notes & caveats

  • Prices are planning estimates for four guests, seven nights, Caribbean, cruise fare only. Cruise pricing moves constantly with date, ship, cabin category and promotions — treat these as ballpark, and confirm with a live quote before deciding.
  • Not included in the fare figures: flights from Kansas City (roughly $1,000–$1,800 for four to Florida or San Juan), any pre-cruise hotel night, shore excursions (except where noted as included, e.g. Regent), and personal spending.
  • Age and cabin rules reflect current published policies for sailings that call at a U.S. port, where "adult" means 21+. Rules differ on itineraries with no U.S. port. Always re-confirm at booking, as lines update these.
  • Weather: Eastern and especially Southern Caribbean routes (e.g. round-trip San Juan) run warmest and most pool-reliable — the opposite of your cool Mexican-Riviera experience.
